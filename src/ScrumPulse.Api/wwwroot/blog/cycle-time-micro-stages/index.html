Splitting cycle time into micro-stages found the bottleneck in a week

Engineering flow7 August 20266 min readRanjitha Savandaiah

Cycle time as normally measured is one number: how long from starting a item to finishing it. When that number is bad, the usual response is to ask the team to focus, which is advice with nowhere to land.

Seven timestamps instead of two

We instrumented the path a work item takes as a sequence of stages rather than a start and an end: backlog, in progress, PR created, PR approved, merged, in QA, done. Each transition writes a timestamp. Nobody fills in a form; the stage changes as part of work people were doing anyway.

The interesting quantity is not the total. It is the gap between consecutive stages, because each gap has a different owner and a different remedy.

What showed up immediately

Development time — in progress to PR created — was fine. It was the number everyone assumed was the problem and it was not.

The gap between PR created and PR approved was several times larger than any other stage. Work was not slow to build. It was slow to be looked at. That is a scheduling problem, not a capability problem, and the interventions for it are completely different: a fixed review window, reviewer assignment at creation, a WIP limit that counts open PRs.

None of that would have been suggested by a single cycle-time figure, and the instinct it does provoke — asking developers to work faster — targets the one stage that was not contributing.

Measure in working hours

A PR raised at five on Friday and approved at nine on Monday took four working hours, not sixty-four. Measuring in wall-clock time makes every Friday afternoon look like a crisis and floods the data with noise that tracks the weekend rather than the team.

Averages hide the thing you want

Review latency averaging four hours sounds healthy until you look at the distribution and find most PRs are reviewed within thirty minutes and a handful sit for two days. The average describes neither group. The long tail is where the delivery risk lives, and an average is specifically designed to make tails disappear.

Look at how many items breached a threshold, not at the mean.

A caution

Per-stage timings are per-item measurements, and it is a very short step from there to per-person measurements. Resist it. The moment review latency is read as a reviewer's score, PRs get approved quickly and read carefully by nobody, and you have optimised away the review itself. Keep the measurement on the work.
