Why we put an eight hour SLA on blockers, and what it changed

Delivery metrics2 September 20266 min readRanjitha Savandaiah

Every team I have worked with tracks blockers. Almost none of them track how long a blocker has been blocking. That distinction sounds pedantic and it is the difference between a list nobody reads and a number that forces a decision.

A status tells you nothing about urgency

When a blocker is a boolean, every blocker looks the same. The one raised an hour ago sits in the same column as the one raised on Tuesday. In standup both get the same fifteen seconds, and the Tuesday one gets them for the fourth day running because nobody has a reason to treat it differently today than yesterday.

What changes the conversation is elapsed time. "Blocked on the client API key, waiting nineteen hours" is a different sentence from "blocked on the client API key". The first one carries its own argument for escalation. Nobody has to make the case.

Why eight hours

We picked eight working hours, which is roughly one working day. The number matters less than the fact that it is shorter than people expect. A threshold of three days gets treated as permission to wait three days. A threshold of one day means a blocker surviving overnight is already a problem, and that is the behaviour we wanted.

Counting working hours rather than wall-clock hours matters more than it sounds. A blocker raised at four on Friday afternoon is not twelve hours old on Saturday morning in any sense a team can act on. Counting only working time keeps the number honest, and it stops Monday morning starting with a screen of false breaches that people learn to ignore.

Categorise by cause, not by severity

We initially tagged blockers by severity and it told us nothing. Severity is a restatement of how loudly the blocked person complained. What produced useful signal was categorising by cause: waiting on client clarification, waiting on a tech lead decision, waiting on environment access, waiting on a third party.

After a few sprints the pattern is usually unambiguous. One category will dominate, and it is almost never the one the team assumed. In our case a third of all blocked hours traced to environment access, which is not an agile problem, not a capacity problem, and not something any amount of standup discipline would have surfaced. It was a permissions process nobody owned.

What it changed

Three things, in order of how much they mattered.

Escalation became unemotional. The clock escalates, not the person. That removes the social cost of saying a colleague or a client is holding you up, which is the real reason blockers go unreported.

Retrospectives got a subject. "How was the sprint" produces vague answers. "We lost forty-one hours to environment access across four blockers" produces an action item.

And the standup got shorter, because a blocker with a visible clock does not need to be re-explained every morning.

The failure mode to watch

An SLA becomes theatre the moment breaching it has no consequence. If blockers routinely sit at forty hours and nothing happens, the number has taught everyone that the number does not matter, and you are worse off than before you measured it. Either the threshold is one somebody acts on, or do not set one.
